# Engagement Scoping, Planning, and Intake

Load at the start of any engagement. Everything here ends up in the report, so
recording it now saves rewriting it later.

---

## The scoping questions

Answer all of these in writing before touching data. If the user cannot answer
one, that is itself worth knowing, and sometimes it is the real finding.

1. **Who is the client?** The entity, the person instructing you, and who will
   read the output. These are often three different answers.
2. **What is the question?** In one sentence, answerable, with a subject and a
   period. "Review the books" is a task, not a question. "Did the 2025 equity
   movement change the owner's basis?" is a question.
3. **What period?** Exact dates. Then name the period immediately before it,
   because the roll-forward needs the prior closing balances.
4. **What triggered this?** A suspicion, a bank request, a partner exit, a tax
   notice, a lawsuit, a routine cycle. The trigger tells you the posture and it
   frequently tells you where to look first.
5. **Who keeps the books, and who reviews them?** If that is the same person,
   write it down now. It is a control finding regardless of what else you find.
6. **What systems and what periods do they cover?** Accounting package, bank
   portals, payroll, point of sale, inventory, expense tools. Note any system
   change during the period, because conversions are where balances go missing.
7. **What is explicitly out of scope?** Write it down and repeat it in the
   report. Undefined scope is how a two-day review becomes a month.
8. **Are there deadlines that are not yours?** A tax filing, a statute, a
   discovery cutoff, a closing date. These change sequencing.
9. **Conflicts.** In any dispute, get the party names before reading records.

## What you cannot do, stated once, plainly

Put this in every deliverable. It protects the user more than it protects you,
because it tells them what they still need to go and get.

> This work is an examination of the records identified in this report. It is
> not an audit, review, or compilation conducted under professional auditing or
> accounting standards, and it expresses no opinion on the financial statements
> taken as a whole. It is not legal advice, tax advice, or a valuation opinion.
> Conclusions that depend on law, tax treatment, or the interpretation of a
> contract should be confirmed with a qualified professional before being acted
> on. Findings are limited to the records produced and the period examined;
> records not produced are identified in the Scope and Limitations section.

Adjust the wording to the engagement. Do not delete it.

## When to hand off to a credentialed professional

You are not required to hold a credential to examine records, reconcile an
account, or tell an owner what their own books say. You **are** required to
know where the line is, and to say so before it is crossed rather than after.

Hand off when the work needs:

| Need | Who |
|---|---|
| An opinion on financial statements (audit, review, compilation) | Licensed **CPA** in the relevant jurisdiction |
| Tax treatment, basis, or filing consequences | **CPA** or tax attorney |
| Whether conduct is unlawful, or what remedy exists | **Attorney** |
| Testimony as an expert in a contested matter | Someone credentialed and experienced in testifying. Common: **CFE** (Certified Fraud Examiner, ACFE), **CFF** (Certified in Financial Forensics, AICPA), **CPA**, **ABV** or **CVA** for valuation |
| A business valuation that will be relied on | **ABV**, **CVA**, or **ASA** credentialed appraiser |
| Device imaging, mailbox review, or anything touching a person's data | Digital forensics specialist, **instructed by counsel** |

**The handoff sentence is short and it is not an apology.** "This is where I
stop. The transactions and the documentation gap are set out above. Whether
that changes your basis is a question for your CPA, and I would take them the
roll-forward schedule." That is more useful than an answer you are not
positioned to give.

Work done properly here does not become worthless at the handoff. It becomes
the thing the credentialed person starts from, which is usually the expensive
part of their engagement.

## Choosing the work: risk-based planning

For a standing or recurring engagement rather than a single question.

### Build the universe first

List everything that *could* be audited: cycles (revenue, purchasing, payroll,
cash, inventory, fixed assets, close and reporting), entities, locations,
systems, and compliance obligations. You are not auditing all of it. You are
making sure nothing important is invisible because nobody ever listed it.

### Rank by impact and likelihood

For each item, ask what it costs if it goes wrong and how likely that is.
Precision is not the point; the ranking is. A rough ranking beats intuition,
and it can be shown to whoever approves the work.

| Band | What the engagement should do |
|---|---|
| High impact, low likelihood | Audit the **controls holding the likelihood down**. Low likelihood is usually a consequence of a control, not a fact of nature. If the control quietly stopped operating, the risk is live and unmonitored. |
| High or medium impact, high or medium likelihood | Go past confirming the exposure. Find the **root cause** and recommend action on the cause. A finding aimed at a symptom produces the same finding next cycle. |
| Low impact, low likelihood | State that it is not covered, and why. Silence reads as coverage. |

### The four engagement types

Most work is one of these. Name which you are doing, because the evidence and
the output differ.

1. **Control monitoring.** Do the controls exist, are they designed to work,
   did they actually operate during the period?
2. **Information examination.** Are the financial and operating numbers
   reliable, and how are they identified, measured, classified, and reported?
   Includes detailed testing of transactions, balances, and procedures.
3. **Value for money.** Economy, efficiency, effectiveness. Are resources
   bought well, used well, and producing the intended result? Largely
   non-financial, and the type most often skipped.
4. **Compliance.** Against external requirements (law, regulation, licence,
   contract, covenant) and internal ones (policy, delegated authority,
   operating agreement). Name which requirement, by section.

### Get the plan approved, and make approval easy

Whoever authorizes the work should see, for each engagement:

- Why this area, and why now rather than later.
- The objectives, and how the scope follows from them.
- How the area is currently monitored, if at all.
- What prior work found, and what is still open from it.
- Current events touching the area: a system change, a new lender, a
  departure, a new line of business.
- What new or continuing exposure the work is designed to address.
- Resource limits, and what will not get done because of them. **State the
  impact of the limit.** An unstated constraint becomes an unexplained gap.

### Re-plan when the facts move

The plan is a hypothesis. When what you find differs materially from what you
assumed, revise the plan rather than completing the original program on
momentum. Re-assess at least annually for standing work, more often if the
business is changing.

Agree at the outset which changes the person doing the work can make alone
(**minor**: a sample size, a sequence, an added test) and which go back for
approval (**major**: scope, cost, timing, or anything that changes what the
conclusions can cover). Put it in writing or every change becomes an argument.

## Document request list

Ask for all of it. You will not get all of it. **What does not arrive is part
of the record**, and an unrecorded gap becomes an unexplained hole later.

### Always

- General ledger for the period, with account numbers and full detail
- Trial balance, opening and closing
- Profit and loss, and balance sheet, for the period
- **The prior period's closing balance sheet and trial balance** (non-negotiable, see the roll-forward)
- Chart of accounts
- Bank statements, all accounts, all months, **as issued by the bank**
- Credit card statements, all cards, all months
- Bank reconciliations as prepared
- Journal entries, especially manual and period-end entries, with support

### Usually

- Prior-year tax returns for the entity and relevant schedules
- 1099s, W-2s, K-1s issued and received
- Accounts payable and receivable aging
- Payroll registers and payroll tax filings
- Loan agreements, notes, and amortization schedules
- Operating agreement, bylaws, partnership agreement, and any amendments
- Minutes, consents, and resolutions, particularly for distributions, loans to
  or from owners, and asset transactions
- Fixed asset register with additions and disposals
- Inventory records and count sheets
- Major contracts and leases

### In a dispute or where the question points there

- Vendor master file, with dates added and by whom
- Signature authority and banking resolutions
- System access and permission logs, and the audit trail or change log
- Email and correspondence for the relevant counterparties and dates
- Related-party schedules and intercompany accounts
- Expense reimbursement reports with receipts
- Insurance policies, claims, and correspondence

## Record what you asked for and what arrived

Keep a request log with: item, date requested, date received, who produced it,
format, and status. Anything still open at report time goes in Scope and
Limitations **by name**, with what it would have let you test.

"Not produced" is a finding when it is a document the business is required to
have. Say which requirement.

## Honest limitations of this kind of work

State these when they apply. They manage expectations and they are true.

- **The audit cannot outrun the accounting.** If the books are not closed, not
  reconciled, or months behind, the examination waits or it examines a moving
  target. Sequence the work accordingly and say so rather than producing
  findings against an incomplete ledger.
- **Findings only matter if someone acts.** This work reports to management or
  the owner, who decide whether to do anything. A finding delivered and ignored
  is a common outcome and it is not a failure of the finding. Track open items
  across cycles so that "ignored" is at least visible.
- **Independence is fragile and easy to lose.** The moment the reviewer starts
  designing the fix, implementing it, or deciding the business's risk appetite,
  they are reviewing their own work next cycle. Advise and challenge. Do not
  decide. See `audit-function-governance.md`.
- **Competence is not general.** A review that touches tax, law, valuation, IT
  systems, or an industry's technical operations needs someone who knows that
  area. Name the gap rather than papering over it.
- **Scope is set by whoever pays.** A narrow scope produces a narrow report,
  and readers routinely treat a narrow report as a broad one. The Scope and
  Limitations section is the only defense, which is why it goes near the front
  and not in an appendix.
