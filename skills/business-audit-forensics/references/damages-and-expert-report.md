# Quantification and the Expert Report

Load when the engagement needs a number, or when anything you write might
reach a court, an insurer, or opposing counsel.

---

## Part 1: Quantification

### Pick the model that answers the theory

The model must match the claim. A model chosen because it is easy to compute,
or because it produces a larger number, is the first thing attacked and the
easiest thing to discredit.

| Model | What it does | Fits when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Specific identification** | Add up the identified transactions | Misappropriation with traceable items | Activity was never recorded |
| **Before and after** | Compare performance before and after the event | The business has a stable history and the event has a clear date | The business was already changing, or the market moved |
| **Yardstick / benchmark** | Compare against a comparable business, location, or line | A credible comparator exists and can be defended | The comparator differs in ways that matter, which it usually does |
| **Discounted cash flow** | Value lost future cash flows | The loss is forward-looking and projections are supportable | Projections rest on assumption stacked on assumption |
| **Cost to correct** | What it costs to put right | The remedy is a defined piece of work | The harm is not fixable by spending |
| **Unjust enrichment** | What the other side gained | The gain is measurable and the theory supports it | Their gain and your loss are unrelated |

**Name why you chose it and why you rejected the alternatives.** That paragraph
is the difference between a defensible model and an arbitrary one.

### Rules that apply to every model

**Tie every input to a source.** Each assumption traces to a document, a
record, or a stated and justified basis. An input with no source is the thread
that unravels the report.

**Test mitigation.** Did the injured party act reasonably to limit the loss?
An unmitigated loss is frequently not recoverable, and ignoring the question
signals you did not think about it.

**Test capacity.** Could the business actually have produced the claimed lost
volume? Capacity, staffing, supply, and working capital all cap a lost-profits
claim, and a claim above physical capacity is not credible.

**Use incremental profit, not revenue.** Lost profit is lost revenue less the
costs that would have been incurred to earn it. Claiming revenue as loss is
the most common overstatement in this work and the most easily attacked.

**Separate the event from everything else.** A market decline, a pandemic, a
lost customer unrelated to the dispute, a management change. If the period
contains other causes, the analysis has to address them, not ignore them.

**State the sensitivity range in the body of the report.** Not in an appendix.
Show what the number becomes under reasonable alternative assumptions. A single
point estimate presented with no range reads as false precision, and giving the
range yourself is far better than having it extracted from you.

**Say what you could not quantify.** A loss you believe exists but cannot
measure is reported as exactly that.

### Interest, taxes, and present value

Say whether the figure includes prejudgment interest, and at what rate and
from what date, or state that it excludes interest. Say whether it is pre-tax
or after-tax. Say whether future amounts are discounted and at what rate, with
the basis for that rate.

**Do not decide the legal questions here.** Whether interest runs, from when,
and at what rate is usually a matter of law or contract. Present the
calculation on a stated assumption and say the assumption is counsel's to
confirm.

## Part 2: The expert standard

### The federal test, as it currently stands

In United States federal courts, expert testimony is governed by **Federal
Rule of Evidence 702**. Verified against the Legal Information Institute
(law.cornell.edu) on 19 September 2026; most recent amendment 2023. Current
text:

> A witness who is qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience,
> training, or education may testify in the form of an opinion or otherwise if
> the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not that:
>
> (a) the expert's scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge will
> help the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in
> issue;
>
> (b) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data;
>
> (c) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and methods; and
>
> (d) the expert's opinion reflects a reliable application of the principles
> and methods to the facts of the case.

**Verify the current text before relying on it in any matter.** Rules are
amended. A quotation in a reference file is a snapshot, and this one carries
its date for exactly that reason.

**State courts differ.** Some follow the federal standard, some apply a
different test, and some have their own codified rule. **Do not assume the
federal test applies.** Confirm the standard in the actual forum, and confirm
it with counsel, not from memory.

### What the test means for how you work

Design the file to satisfy it from day one, because the decision to testify
comes later and the file cannot be rebuilt retroactively.

**Sufficient facts or data.** Enough of the right records, not a convenient
subset. If you worked from a sample, say so, say why the sample is adequate,
and say how it was selected. If records were withheld, say which and what you
could not test as a result.

**Reliable principles and methods.** Methods recognized in the field, and a
stated reason for choosing each one. Where you departed from a standard
approach, say so and justify it. A bespoke method invented for one case is not
automatically unreliable, but it carries the burden of explanation.

**Reliable application.** The transformation log, the workpapers, the
parameters, the data dictionary. Someone else must be able to re-perform your
work and get your numbers.

**Helpfulness.** The opinion has to assist a non-expert. An analysis nobody can
follow does not help, however correct it is.

### The overstatement trap

The 2023 amendment tightened the focus on experts claiming more certainty than
their method supports. The practical rule:

**Never state a conclusion at a confidence your method cannot carry.** Not in
the opinion, not in the summary, not in a chart title, and not under
questioning. If the method supports "the records produced do not document a
business purpose for these transfers," then that is the sentence, and any
stronger version of it is a liability.

## Part 3: The report

### Structure

1. **Assignment.** Who engaged you, when, and what you were asked to determine.
   Word for word, not a paraphrase.
2. **Qualifications.** Relevant experience only. Prior testimony where the
   forum requires it.
3. **Information considered.** Everything relied on, itemized, cross-referenced
   to the evidence register. **And what was requested and not produced.**
4. **Scope and limitations.** Near the front, never buried. Period covered,
   records excluded, assumptions made, what the work is not. This is the
   section that stops a narrow review being read as a broad one.
5. **Background.** The business, the parties, the events. Plain narrative.
6. **Methodology.** What you did, in the order you did it, in enough detail to
   be re-performed.
7. **Findings.** Each one stated with its evidence, its source, and its
   provenance tag. Organized so a reader can follow without jumping.
8. **Quantification.** The model, why it was chosen, the inputs and their
   sources, the result, the sensitivity range.
9. **Opinions.** Only if the engagement calls for opinions, and only within the
   limits of the method. Each opinion tied to the findings supporting it.
10. **Exhibits.** Timelines, flow diagrams, schedules, key documents.
11. **Signature and date.**

### Writing rules

**Write for an intelligent non-accountant.** A judge, a juror, an insurer, an
owner, a spouse. This is not simplification; the ability to make financial
material understandable without distorting it is the core professional skill,
and the expert-witness role is the public test of it.

- Short sentences. One idea each.
- The plainest precise word. Terms of art stay where they are precise; ordinary
  ideas do not get dressed up.
- Define any term the first time it is used.
- Flow diagram labels in plain words: "From the company, to the vendor, to a
  personal account." Not "Dr / Cr."
- **No em dashes.** Commas, periods, or restructure.
- Dates in prose: comma after the year, including attributive. "the July 7,
  2025, transfer."
- Active voice with a named actor where the actor is known and the statement is
  factual. Passive where the actor is the disputed question.

### The language discipline, restated because it is the thing most often broken

| Do not write | Write |
|---|---|
| "He embezzled $40,000." | "$40,000 moved from the operating account to an account in his name. No invoice, approval, or repayment appears in the records produced." |
| "The books were falsified." | "Fourteen manual entries were posted to the closed prior period after the statements had been issued. Each reduced reported expense." |
| "This is clearly fraud." | "These transactions share the following characteristics. Whether they were authorized is a question the documents produced do not answer." |
| "The bookkeeper was stealing." | "Bank reconciliations were prepared by the person who posted cash entries, with no independent review, throughout the period." |
| "No other issues were found." | "The tests listed in Section 6 were applied to the population described in Section 4. Those tests produced no further exceptions. Tests not applied are listed in Section 5." |

The right-hand column is not weaker. It is the version that survives.

### The summary carries the same provenance as the body

The executive summary is what gets read, quoted, forwarded, and acted on.
A caveat on page 14 does not protect a claim on page 2.

Before finalizing, read only the summary and ask whether every sentence in it
is true at the strength stated. Anything that is not either gets its qualifier
back or comes out.

### Rebuttal and the other side's report

When reviewing an opposing analysis, test it the way yours will be tested:

- What records did they rely on, and what did they not have?
- Does the model fit their own theory?
- Which inputs are sourced and which are assumed?
- Do the arithmetic and the schedules actually tie?
- What did they exclude, and why?
- Is any conclusion stated at a confidence the method cannot support?

**Where they are right, say so.** A rebuttal that disputes everything is
discounted entirely. Conceding the defensible parts is what gives weight to the
parts you genuinely contest.
